Three Landmarks in Jayanta Mahapatra’s Poetic Geography : Kathjodi, Mahanadi and Chandrabhaga 

Pradeep Biswal 
Legendary poet Jayanta Mahapatra’s poetry grows out of Odisha’s physical landscape, yet it is never merely descriptive. Rivers in his work—especially Kathjodi, Mahanadi, and Chandrabhaga—function as moral witnesses, bearing silent testimony to memory, deprivation, faith, and death. Through them, Mahapatra constructs a poetic geography where place becomes conscience. His house in Tinikonia Bagicha, Cuttack was situated in almost equal distance from Kathjodi and Mahanadi  which were associated with his childhood memories. He named his house as Chandrabhaga in the name of river Chandrabhaga near Sun temple Konark. He therefore shared an intimate relationship with these three rivers contributing to his poetic journey.

Kathjodi:The River of Private Memory

Kathjodi, a branch of river Mahanadi , flowing quietly through Cuttack, belongs to Mahapatra’s intimate, inward world. Though rarely named with rhetorical emphasis, the river is felt as a presence of stillness, familiarity, and emotional restraint. It aligns with the poet’s recurring tone of muted recollection. Mahapatra’s characteristic evocation of such landscapes appears in “A Rain of Rites”, where the riverine space is inseparable from memory and hesitation:
“The past lies everywhere like water.”
This line captures the essence of Kathjodi in Mahapatra’s poetry—unassertive yet pervasive, shaping consciousness rather than announcing itself. The river becomes an extension of the poet’s interiority, where childhood, loss, and belonging coexist without resolution. Kathjodi is thus the river of lived memory, flowing through the poet’s personal history rather than myth or spectacle.

Mahanadi: History, Hunger  and Silence

If Kathjodi is inward, the Mahanadi is historical and collective. Mahapatra repeatedly associates Odisha’s rivers with famine, poverty, and abandonment, especially in poems that recall colonial exploitation and postcolonial neglect.

In the poem “The Whorehouse in a Calcutta Street” and several other Odisha-centered poems, Mahapatra evokes the landscape as burdened with human suffering rather than promise. A stark line from his much acclaimed poem “Hunger” resonates strongly with the river’s symbolic role:

“It was hard to believe the flesh was heavy on my back.”
Though not naming the Mahanadi directly, such lines echo the river’s presence as a carrier of bodily hunger and historical weight. The Mahanadi flows through regions where survival itself becomes a daily ordeal, and Mahapatra allows the river to remain silent—never redemptive, never consoling. The river does not fail the people; history does. 

Chandrabhaga: Ritual, Faith and Irony

The Chandrabhaga, near Konark, is Mahapatra’s most explicitly symbolic river. Associated with ritual bathing during Magha Saptami, it represents the collision between faith and mortality. In his celebrated poem “Dawn at Puri”, Mahapatra presents one of the most haunting ritual landscapes in Indian English poetry:

“A skull on the holy sands
tilts its empty country towards hunger.”
This image undercuts the promise of ritual purification. The sacred setting—linked culturally and geographically to Chandrabhaga and Puri—becomes a space where death intrudes upon devotion. The river witnesses bodies not renewed, but exhausted. Another line from the same poem reinforces this irony:

“The wet air tiring of incense
brings a chill of silence.”
Here, religious performance is rendered fragile, almost futile, against the immensity of human suffering. Chandrabhaga thus emerges as a river where belief is enacted but never answered. The river which is physically extinct now serves as a symbol of life’s futility. 

Silence : A Shared Current

Silence is always an undercurrent of Mahapatra’s poetry. Across Kathjodi, Mahanadi, and Chandrabhaga, the dominant motif is silence. Mahapatra’s rivers do not speak; they endure. This aligns with his restrained poetic voice, which refuses grand consolation. In the poem “Bare Face of Night”, Mahapatra writes:

“Silence is what I hear.”
This line could stand as an epigraph to his river imagery. The rivers absorb prayers, hunger, and grief, but offer no response. They are ethical landscapes, not lyrical ornaments.

Though rooted in Odisha, these rivers transcend regional specificity. Kathjodi becomes the river of personal memory, Mahanadi of historical injustice, and Chandrabhaga of rituals confronting death. Together, they chart a poetic terrain where geography becomes destiny.

Mahapatra’s rivers remind us that poetry, like water, carries what history leaves behind. In Jayanta Mahapatra’s poetry, Kathjodi, Mahanadi, and Chandrabhaga are not symbols imposed on landscape; they are living landscapes that think. Flowing through his poems , they gather memory, suffering, and faith into a quiet but relentless moral presence. As Mahapatra shows us, rivers do not explain They too remember. They are a part of our collective consciousness.
