Country as a Metaphor in Hara Prasad Das’s Desha
Pradeep Biswal
In his modern Odia epic poem Desha, Hara Prasad Das uses the idea of the country not merely as a geographical entity but as a powerful metaphor for collective suffering, memory, and moral decline. Here, Desha (country) functions as a layered metaphor that represents memory, identity, history, suffering, and moral responsibility. The word Desha itself becomes symbolic, expanding beyond borders to represent the lived reality of the people. The poem repeatedly presents the country as physically injured and exhausted, reflecting centuries of exploitation and neglect. The poem transforms the idea of the nation into a living being with an emotionally charged presence. One of the most powerful metaphorical usage used by the poet in Desha is the personification of the country as a human body.
“This country walks with wounds on its feet,
yet no one asks where it is going.”
Here, the wounded body of the nation symbolises the pain of ordinary people. By giving the country bodily attributes, the poet makes national sufferings quite intimate and unavoidable. The land appears wounded, exhausted, and scarred, suggesting the historical trauma of colonialism, poverty and violence.The metaphor suggests that the country continues to move forward despite suffering, but without care or direction from its citizens. The land in Desha absorbs history and remembers what people choose to forget. The reader is encouraged to feel the pain of the country, not just understand it intellectually. The nations injuries mirror the injuries of its own people.

“The roads know the weight of blood,
even when our mouths refuse to speak.”
This line turns the country into a keeper of suppressed memories. The metaphor implies that historical violence and injustice are embedded in the landscape itself.

The poet often presents the country as silently judging human actions.
“The country watches—
not in anger,
but in endless waiting.”
The restrained tone emphasises that Desha does not protest loudly; instead, its silence becomes an accusation. The metaphor shifts blame from abstract forces to human behaviour—corruption, indifference, and moral decay.
Rather than glorifying the nation, the poem exposes its contradictions.
“One country,
many hungers,
many shadows.”

This fragmented image of Desha reflects social inequality and disunity. The country exists as a shared space, but not a shared experience.
Unlike traditional patriotic poetry, Desha refuses to idealize the nation.
“This is not a holy land,
this is a lived land.”
Here, the metaphor strips the country of myth and sentimentality, grounding it in reality. Hara Prasad urges readers to engage with the nation ethically rather than emotionally. In Desha , the country becomes a repository of memory. Rivers, fields, villages, and streets carry the weight of forgotten history. The land remembers what people try to forget - betrayals, struggles and sacrifices. Here , Desha is not frozen time; it is a palimpsest  layered with past and present simultaneously. The poem suggests that forgetting history is itself a moral failure on the part of its people.
      The poet uses the metaphor of the country to act as a silent judge of its own citizens.The nation watches human action - corruption, apathy. Violence with quiet endurance, The country does not accuse loudly, but its silence becomes more disturbing than protest. Here, problem is not the land . The  problem is is how the people treat their country.
       Most significantly, the poet consciously avoids romantic nationalism. The country is not idealised as a sacred mother or mythic hero. Instead, it is ordinary, damaged, and real.This makes Desha a humanist poem rather than a patriotic one. The metaphor urges readers to engage with the country ethically , not emotionally or blindly.
Through vivid metaphor and restrained language, the poet poet transforms Desha into a wounded body, a bearer of historical memory, a moral witness, and a fractured social reality. The poem ultimately challenges readers to rethink what it means to belong to a country—not as passive inheritors, but as responsible moral agents within it.
In his magnum opus Desha, poet Hara Prasad Das uses the country as a complex , evolving metaphor, a wounded body, a keeper of memory, a moral witness , and a fragmented identity. Through this metaphor , the poem challenges readers to rethink their relationship with the nation  - not based on owners or worshippers, but as responsible participants in its ongoing story. It echoes the eternal voice of the country in the shape of a living organism. Finally, Desha emerges as collective consciousness of its people.
